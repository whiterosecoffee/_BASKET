## Economic interpretation
Scarce delivered compute and falling public equity prices can coexist. Buyers of compute pay for service availability; equity investors value the cash remaining after equipment funding, parent obligations, time delays and dilution. Higher service prices improve future operating returns, while reduced confidence in access to capital can simultaneously reduce the value attributed to those returns.

The article provides cross-company evidence against a broad collapse in demand/pricing. Combined with CRWV's own signed-contract pricing, it supports a hypothesis that much of the stock decline reflects financing confidence and required-return repricing. It does not identify today's bond movements or prove the entire equity decline is unwarranted.

Capacity restrictions at competing providers can support pricing and incumbency advantages for operators with equipment and power already secured. They do not eliminate the need to fund CRWV's next deployment or establish that existing fixed customer contracts can be repriced immediately.

## Transparent sensitivity, not a CRWV forecast
For a hypothetical contract cohort earning $1B annually, a 5% revenue-price increase contributes $37.5M if the increment has a 75% contribution rate. A three-percentage-point interest increase on $1B of affected average debt costs $30M annually. Pricing can offset that illustrative increase **if** enough of the cohort's revenue is available to reprice and incremental delivery costs do not absorb the gain.

The contribution rate, debt/revenue ratio and price change here are assumptions. Keep existing fixed coupons unchanged, apply rate shocks only to affected debt, and respect customer acceptance and contract reset dates.

## What would distinguish an opportunity from justified repricing?
1. Signed service economics remain strong while long-term funding is secured at viable cost: supports a temporary confidence discount.
2. Delivery/billing catches up with already-incurred expenditure: converts the scarcity argument into attributable cash.
3. Equity issuance or expensive parent funding consumes a disproportionate share of project returns: good demand can still produce weak per-share outcomes.
4. Contract prices, renewals or customer payment performance deteriorate: undermines the operating case, rather than merely investor sentiment.
